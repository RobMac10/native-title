Numbers checked on 20 November 2026.

In 1982 a group of Meriam people went to the High Court to prove that their island was theirs. Ten years later the Court agreed. In 1993 the Parliament made the Native Title Act. In 1996 the Court answered the Wik peoples’ questions about pastoral leases, and in 1998 the Parliament amended the Act. In 2007 the Commonwealth passed laws for the Northern Territory, and in 2008 the Parliament apologised to the Stolen Generations.

How this works

What did they say? Could they really know? Why might they say it? “We can’t know” is always an allowed answer, if you can say why.

    Equal before the law, 1975

    What the paper said

    Racial Discrimination Act 1975 · section 9

    “9. (1) It is unlawful for a person to do any act involving a distinction, exclusion, restriction or preference based on race, colour, descent or national or ethnic origin which has the purpose or effect of nullifying or impairing the recognition, enjoyment or exercise, on an equal footing, of any human right or fundamental freedom in the political, economic, social, cultural or any other field of public life.”

    Source. Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth), No. 52 of 1975, s. 9. Federal Register of Legislation, as made.

    See the source (Federal Register of Legislation) ↗

    What the records say happened

    What happened · 1985 and 1988

    In 1985 the Queensland Parliament passed a law that cancelled Torres Strait Islanders’ claims to their traditional lands. In 1988 the High Court overturned it, because it conflicted with the Racial Discrimination Act. The Meriam people’s case could go on.

    Source. National Museum of Australia, Digital Classroom, “Mabo decision” (secondhand; the 1985 Act and the 1988 judgment were not seen). A summary, not a quotation.

    Section 9 made racial discrimination unlawful. When a State law took away one group’s land claims, did section 9 protect them?

    Does the record match the paper?

    “So low in the scale”, 1919 and 1992

    What the paper said

    Privy Council · In re Southern Rhodesia · 1919

    “Some tribes are so low in the scale of social organization that their usages and conceptions of rights and duties are not to be reconciled with the institutions or the legal ideas of civilized society.”

    Source. Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992) 175 CLR 1, Brennan J, quoting In re Southern Rhodesia [1919] AC 211.

    What the records say happened

    Justice Brennan · Mabo v Queensland (No 2) · 3 June 1992

    “The fiction by which the rights and interests of indigenous inhabitants in land were treated as non-existent was justified by a policy which has no place in the contemporary law of this country.”

    Source. Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992) 175 CLR 1, Brennan J.

    See the judgment (AustLII) ↗

    Earlier law rested on the 1919 view. Did the High Court in 1992 keep it?

    Wik, 1996 and 1998

    What the paper said

    The High Court · Wik Peoples v Queensland · 23 December 1996

    “(b) does the pastoral lease confer rights to exclusive possession on the grantee?”
    Answer: “(b) No.”

    Source. Wik Peoples v Queensland (1996) 187 CLR 1, answers to Question 1B.

    See the judgment (AustLII) ↗

    What the records say happened

    Native Title Act 1993, as amended in 1998 · section 4(6)

    “This Act also confirms that many acts done before the High Court’s judgment, that were either valid, or have been validated under the past act or intermediate period act provisions, will have extinguished native title.”

    Words on the record

    The people who wrote the laws and the reports chose their words. Some words hide more than they show. Tap each card to decode it.

    • Latin for “land belonging to no-one”. The Native Title Act’s preamble records that the High Court “rejected the doctrine that Australia was terra nullius” at the time of European settlement.

      Source. Native Title Act 1993, preamble.

    • The rights and interests in land that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples hold under their own laws and customs, recognised by Australian law. Section 10: “Native title is recognised, and protected, in accordance with this Act.”

      Source. Native Title Act 1993, s. 10.

    • Law made by judges’ decisions over time, not by Parliament. Justice Brennan said it should not be “frozen in an age of racial discrimination”.

      Source. Mabo v Queensland (No 2), Brennan J.

    • Something treated as true in law even though it is not. Justice Brennan called the treatment of indigenous rights as “non-existent” a fiction.

      Source. Mabo v Queensland (No 2), Brennan J.

    • A right that holds against everyone, not just one other party. The Court declared the Meriam people entitled “as against the whole world” to Mer, with exceptions.

      Source. Mabo v Queensland (No 2), Brennan J.

    • Wiped out for good. The preamble says native title is extinguished by valid government acts inconsistent with it, “such as the grant of freehold or leasehold estates”.

      Source. Native Title Act 1993, preamble.

    • A government lease of land for grazing stock. The Wik question was whether such a lease gave “exclusive possession”.

      Source. Wik Peoples v Queensland (1996).

    • The right to keep everyone else off the land. The Court answered that the Holroyd River lease did not give it.

      Source. Wik Peoples v Queensland (1996), answers.

    • A word the Act uses for the needs of “the broader Australian community”.

      Source. Native Title Act 1993, preamble.

    • Under the Convention set out in the Racial Discrimination Act, measures “taken for the sole purpose of securing adequate advancement of certain racial or ethnic groups”. Part II of the Act does not apply to them (s. 8). Section 132 of the 2007 Act declared its provisions to be special measures.

      Source. Racial Discrimination Act 1975, s. 8 and Schedule, Art. 1(4); Northern Territory National Emergency Response Act 2007, s. 132.

    • The Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people removed as children from their families. The motion apologised for “the pain, suffering and hurt of these Stolen Generations”.

      Source. PM Transcripts, transcript 15758.

    Mabo, 1982 to 1992

    Why did the High Court decide that the Meriam people held their land?

    What is agreed

    • In May 1982 a group of Meriam people from Mer, in the eastern Torres Strait, began a case in the High Court for the ownership of their island. They were led by Eddie Koiki Mabo (National Museum of Australia).
    • Over ten years, thirty-three Meriam people researched and wrote 4,000 pages of evidence and argument. It showed that the eight clans of Mer had lived in clearly defined areas of the island for hundreds of years (NMA).
    • Eddie Mabo died a few months before the case was decided (NMA).
    • On 3 June 1992 six of the seven judges agreed that the Meriam people held their land.

    Ask three witnesses

    Ask every witness the same three questions: What did they say? Could they really know? Why might they say it? Some witnesses here are documents, not people. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

    Eddie Koiki Mabo

    As quoted by the National Museum of Australia

    Source. National Museum of Australia, Digital Classroom, “Mabo decision”. Secondhand: when and where he said it is not given there.

    See the source (NMA) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “My father told me: “Son, this land will belong to you when I die.””

    Could they really have seen it? He was Meriam and grew up on Mer. He is reporting what his father told him.

    Why might they say it? He led the case. His father’s words describe the Meriam law of inheritance that the case set out to prove.

    Justice Brennan

    Mabo v Queensland (No 2) · 3 June 1992

    Source. Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992) 175 CLR 1, Brennan J.

    See the judgment (AustLII) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “it is imperative in today’s world that the common law should neither be nor be seen to be frozen in an age of racial discrimination.”

    “The fiction by which the rights and interests of indigenous inhabitants in land were treated as non-existent was justified by a policy which has no place in the contemporary law of this country.”

    Could they really have seen it? He was one of the seven judges, deciding the case on the evidence and the law. He did not see Mer’s history himself; he read the evidence.

    Why might they say it? A judge must give reasons. Here he explains why the Court would not follow earlier law.

    Justice Dawson

    Mabo v Queensland (No 2) · 3 June 1992 · the one dissent

    Source. Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992) 175 CLR 1, Dawson J.

    See the judgment (AustLII) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “My conclusion that the plaintiffs have no aboriginal title to the land necessarily carries with it the further conclusion that the plaintiffs' separate claim to usufructuary rights over the land cannot succeed.”

    Could they really have seen it? He heard the same case and read the same evidence, and reached the opposite answer.

    Why might they say it? A dissenting judge explains why he disagrees. His reasons are part of the record, even though he lost.

    The Native Title Act, 1993

    What did the Native Title Act protect, and what did it confirm?

    What is agreed

    • After the Mabo judgment, the Commonwealth Parliament made a law for native title.
    • The Native Title Act received assent on 24 December 1993.
    • It opens with a preamble setting out what the Parliament took into account.

    Ask three witnesses

    Ask every witness the same three questions: What did they say? Could they really know? Why might they say it? Some witnesses here are documents, not people. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

    The preamble, on the past

    Native Title Act 1993 · preamble

    Source. Native Title Act 1993 (Cth), No. 110 of 1993, preamble.

    See the source (Federal Register of Legislation) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “The people whose descendants are now known as Aboriginal peoples and Torres Strait Islanders were the inhabitants of Australia before European settlement.”

    “They have been progressively dispossessed of their lands. This dispossession occurred largely without compensation” …

    Could they really have seen it? It is the Parliament speaking. A preamble is not itself a rule; it explains why the rules follow.

    Why might they say it? It names dispossession and the lack of compensation, in an Act of the Commonwealth.

    The preamble, on the law

    Native Title Act 1993 · preamble

    Source. Native Title Act 1993, preamble.

    See the source ↗

    What did they say they saw? “(c) held that native title is extinguished by valid government acts that are inconsistent with the continued existence of native title rights and interests, such as the grant of freehold or leasehold estates.”

    “The needs of the broader Australian community require certainty and the enforceability of acts potentially made invalid because of the existence of native title.”

    Could they really have seen it? It summarises what the High Court held, and states what the Parliament thought the wider community needed.

    Why might they say it? Look at the order: recognition first, then extinguishment, then “certainty”. Who needed certainty?

    Section 10, and the objects

    Native Title Act 1993 · ss. 3 and 10

    Source. Native Title Act 1993, ss. 3(a) and 10.

    See the source ↗

    What did they say they saw? “10. Native title is recognised, and protected, in accordance with this Act.”

    The main objects include “(a) to provide for the recognition and protection of native title”.

    Could they really have seen it? These are rules, not reasons. They say what the Act does.

    Why might they say it? Recognition is “in accordance with this Act”. The rest of the Act decides how native title is proved and when it gives way.

    Wik, 1996 to 1998

    Could native title survive on a pastoral lease?

    What is agreed

    • The Wik and Thayorre peoples of Cape York, in Queensland, claimed native title over land that had been leased for grazing: the Holroyd River and Mitchellton leases.
    • The question for the High Court was whether a pastoral lease gave the leaseholder the right to exclusive possession, and so wiped out native title.
    • On 23 December 1996 the Court gave its answers. The judges divided.
    • The Native Title Amendment Act 1998 received assent on 27 July 1998.

    Ask three witnesses

    Ask every witness the same three questions: What did they say? Could they really know? Why might they say it? Some witnesses here are documents, not people. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

    The Court’s answers

    Question 1B, on the Holroyd River lease · 23 December 1996

    Source. Wik Peoples v Queensland (1996) 187 CLR 1, orders.

    See the judgment (AustLII) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “(b) does the pastoral lease confer rights to exclusive possession on the grantee?”
    Answer: “(b) No.”

    “(d) did the grant of the pastoral lease necessarily extinguish all incidents of Aboriginal title or possessory title of the Wik Peoples in respect of the land demised under the pastoral lease?”
    Answer: “(d) Strictly does not arise but is properly answered No.”

    Could they really have seen it? These are the Court’s formal answers, the part that binds.

    Why might they say it? Short answers hide long disagreements. The reasons are in the judgments.

    Chief Justice Brennan

    Wik · 1996 · dissenting

    Source. Wik Peoples v Queensland (1996) 187 CLR 1, Brennan CJ.

    See the judgment (AustLII) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “For the reasons stated, the lessees had the right of exclusive possession and that right was inconsistent with native title (except for non-accessory rights, if any) and, as the right of exclusive possession was conferred on the lessees by the Crown as the sovereign power, that right prevailed and the rights of the holders of native title were extinguished.”

    Could they really have seen it? Four years earlier, in Mabo, Chief Justice Mason and Justice McHugh had agreed with his reasons. Here he reached the opposite answer to the majority.

    Why might they say it? He was applying the rule that native title gives way where it is inconsistent with a grant by the Crown.

    Justice Toohey

    Wik · 1996 · in the majority

    Source. Wik Peoples v Queensland (1996) 187 CLR 1, Toohey J.

    See the judgment (AustLII) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “To say that the pastoral leases in question did not confer rights to exclusive possession on the grantees is in no way destructive of the title of those grantees.”

    “Those rights are then measured against the rights conferred on the grantees of the pastoral leases; to the extent of any inconsistency the latter prevail.”

    Could they really have seen it? He read the same leases and Acts as the Chief Justice.

    Why might they say it? He was answering a fear: that recognising native title would take something from the leaseholders.

    The Intervention and the Apology, 2007 to 2008

    What did the Commonwealth say, in 2007 and in 2008?

    What is agreed

    • On 17 August 2007 the Northern Territory National Emergency Response Act received assent. It applied to prescribed areas of the Northern Territory, among them the Aboriginal communities listed in its first schedule.
    • Its Parts dealt with alcohol, publicly funded computers, five-year leases of land, business management areas, bail and sentencing, community stores and income management.
    • On 13 February 2008 the Prime Minister moved a motion of apology in the House of Representatives.

    Ask three witnesses

    Ask every witness the same three questions: What did they say? Could they really know? Why might they say it? Some witnesses here are documents, not people. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

    The 2007 Act, its object

    Northern Territory National Emergency Response Act 2007 · section 5

    Source. Northern Territory National Emergency Response Act 2007 (Cth), No. 129 of 2007, s. 5.

    See the source (Federal Register of Legislation) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “The object of this Act is to improve the well-being of certain communities in the Northern Territory.”

    Could they really have seen it? It is the Parliament stating the Act’s purpose, in its own words.

    Why might they say it? An object clause tells courts how to read the rest of the Act.

    The 2007 Act, section 132

    Northern Territory National Emergency Response Act 2007 · section 132

    Source. Northern Territory National Emergency Response Act 2007, s. 132.

    See the source ↗

    What did they say they saw? “(1) The provisions of this Act, and any acts done under or for the purposes of those provisions, are, for the purposes of the Racial Discrimination Act 1975, special measures.”

    “(2) The provisions of this Act, and any acts done under or for the purposes of those provisions, are excluded from the operation of Part II of the Racial Discrimination Act 1975.”

    Could they really have seen it? It is the law. It states how the Act relates to the Racial Discrimination Act.

    Why might they say it? Compare it with section 9 of the Racial Discrimination Act, in Orders and outcomes.

    The Apology

    House of Representatives · 13 February 2008

    Source. Apology to Australia’s Indigenous Peoples, motion moved by the Prime Minister. PM Transcripts, transcript 15758.

    See the source (PM Transcripts) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “We apologise for the laws and policies of successive Parliaments and governments that have inflicted profound grief, suffering and loss on these our fellow Australians.”

    “For the pain, suffering and hurt of these Stolen Generations, their descendants and for their families left behind, we say sorry.”

    Could they really have seen it? It is a motion of the Parliament. It speaks for the Parliament and the nation.

    Why might they say it? It answers Bringing Them Home (1997), which the Taken investigation explores.
